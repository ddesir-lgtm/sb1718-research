What the data shows

Each finding comes from a different data source and method. Their convergence is the argument.

01
Aggregate null
Employment and unemployment tracked Florida trends. No collapse. System reorganized, did not contract.
02

Establishments +16% in Manatee. Workers per firm: 8.47 to 7.94. Liability-driven structural break.
03
H-2B surge
Sarasota: 253 (FY19) to 535 (FY22) certified positions. Surge before the law passed.

Community stayed
Foreign-born share rose 12.5% to 12.9% in Sarasota. No displacement. Harm happened in place.
The overarching story: SB1718 created a legal liability shock for employers. Firms responded by fragmenting employment into small subcontracting LLCs and pre-emptively building H-2B pipelines. Both responses preserved employment counts while concentrating legal protection upward and economic vulnerability downward. Workers at the bottom of the new structure have less wage theft recourse, shorter employment tenure, and no employer of record with real assets.

How to read the dose-response models: Each county receives an exposure score based on its pre-law foreign-born share and construction employment share. If SB1718 drove the findings, outcomes should scale with that score across all 67 counties -- not just in Sarasota and Manatee. The employment coefficient (-0.008, p<0.001) means a one-unit increase in exposure is associated with 0.8% lower construction payroll employment. The placebo test at a fake 2021 treatment date finds nothing (-0.0007, n.s.), confirming the 2023 signal is not a pre-existing trend.
Plain-language Q&A
How we got here and what it means

Answers to the questions we get most often about the analysis, the data, and the implications.

Core finding

Two simultaneous responses. First, fragmentation: construction establishment counts grew 15.6% in Sarasota and 16.6% in Manatee between 2018 and 2023 while average crew size fell in Manatee from 8.47 to 7.94 workers. The proliferation of small subcontracting LLCs allows lead contractors to distance themselves from direct E-Verify liability. When work is assigned to a four-person subcontractor, the lead contractor is not the employer of record and bears no compliance responsibility at that tier.

Second, authorized substitution: H-2B certified positions in Sarasota grew 111% from FY2019 to FY2022, with the surge concentrated in the two years before the law passed. Firms were anticipating the law, not reacting to it. The dose-response regression confirms both patterns scale with county-level exposure (H-2B positions: beta=+21.47, p<0.001; mean establishment size: beta=-0.46, p<0.001).

Core finding

Employment counts measure whether jobs exist. They do not measure what those jobs protect. A worker employed directly by a general contractor has wage theft recourse against a named employer with real assets, access to workers' compensation, and collective voice. A worker employed by a three-person LLC at the fourth tier of a subcontracting chain has none of those things -- the LLC can dissolve overnight.

The full-quarter employment share result captures this directly: in higher-exposure counties, the share of workers in stable, long-tenure employment fell significantly (beta=-0.0046, p<0.001), even as total employment counts grew. That is the fragmentation effect showing up in an entirely different empirical window from a different data source (QWI rather than BDS).

Core finding

H-2B is a federal guest worker visa for temporary non-agricultural work. Workers are tied to a specific employer by visa terms, cannot easily change jobs if mistreated, and have limited legal recourse outside their sponsoring employer. Sarasota's certified positions: 253 (FY2019), 210 (FY2020), 338 (FY2021), 535 (FY2022). The 58% jump in FY2022 happened the year before the law passed.

This directly contests SB1718's stated rationale. The law was justified as protecting American workers from competition with undocumented immigrants. The data shows the primary substitution effect was replacing undocumented workers with H-2B workers -- a different foreign workforce, under more restrictive visa conditions, serving employer interests more directly. The regression confirms this scales with exposure across all Florida counties (beta=+21.47, p<0.001).

For advocacy: the argument is no longer immigrant workers vs. American workers. It is a documented case that the law's primary labor market beneficiary was the H-2B employer infrastructure. That framing can reach audiences that conventional immigrant advocacy framing cannot: labor rights organizations focused on guest worker exploitation, business groups that find H-2B certification burdensome, and legislators whose enforcement politics are driven by worker protection rather than nativism.

Methodology

From a difference-in-differences on ACS Table B23025 (labor force participation status). Labor force participation in treated counties grew 0.5 percentage points from the pre-treatment average (2019-2022) to 2023. The Florida donor average grew 0.8 percentage points over the same period. The DiD gap is -0.58 percentage points. Applied to Sarasota's estimated working-age population of approximately 380,000, that implies roughly 2,200 people who would have been expected to remain in the labor force based on statewide trends but did not. These workers never register in unemployment statistics because they stop looking.

Confidence level: the LFP gap is real in the data and consistent with the discouraged worker mechanism documented in Arizona SB1070 and Georgia HB87 literature. However, the ACS is a 5-year rolling estimate that attenuates true effects, and the specific mechanism (enforcement fear vs. informal economy shifts vs. differential demographic aging in a coastal retirement community) cannot be confirmed without qualitative evidence. Treat 2,200 as an order-of-magnitude estimate grounded in real data, not a precise count.

Addressed skepticism

This is the most serious alternative explanation and we tested it carefully. Three responses. First, the synthetic control constructs a weighted Florida counterfactual specifically to account for statewide trends -- the question is whether Sarasota and Manatee deviated from comparable counties, not simply whether they grew. Second, a genuine construction boom would produce rising or stable average firm size as demand pulls workers into existing firms. We observe the opposite: more firms with fewer workers per firm. That divergence from boom-predicted behavior is the fragmentation signature. Third, the dose-response test shows higher-exposure counties added proportionally more micro-firms. A uniform statewide boom would produce equal growth across exposure levels.

Honest caveat: the dose-response establishment coefficient is positive but not statistically significant (beta=+0.0006, p=0.71). The 2024 BDS data, when released, will be the cleanest test of whether fragmentation deepened after full implementation.

Addressed skepticism

Ian struck Lee County in September 2022, immediately before the treatment period. We ran the explicit Ian robustness check: removing Lee, Charlotte, DeSoto, and Collier counties from the donor pool entirely. The core fragmentation finding in Sarasota and Manatee survived the exclusion without material change. Ian's rebuilding demand was geographically concentrated in Lee County, which is a distinct labor market from Sarasota and Manatee. The establishment growth we document is within the treated counties themselves, and the donor pool exclusion test confirms it is not an artifact of Ian contaminating the counterfactual.

Data

Census Business Dynamics Statistics (BDS): Annual county-by-NAICS panel on employment, establishment counts, job creation, and destruction. Coverage 2018-2023. Fragmentation analysis, DiD, size distribution.

Census Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI): Q1 employment snapshots by county. Coverage 2019-2025. Extends window beyond BDS cutoff; provides full-quarter stable employment share.

BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics (LAUS): Annual unemployment rates by county. Coverage 2019-2024. Unemployment DiD.

ACS 5-Year Estimates B05002 and B23025: Foreign-born population share and labor force participation status. Coverage 2019-2023. Community stability and discouraged worker analysis.

DOL OFLC H-2B Disclosure Data: Certified H-2B positions by county and employer. Coverage FY2019-FY2022. Authorized substitution analysis and H-2B dose-response regression.

Honest limits

Mechanism behind the LFP gap. The -0.58pp divergence is real in the data. Whether enforcement fear, informal economy shifts, or differential demographic aging caused it cannot be determined from ACS alone. Structured community interviews are the right next step -- only UnidosNow can conduct them credibly given its trust relationships.

Whether fragmentation deepened in 2024. BDS runs through 2023, the first year of full implementation. The 2024 release will show whether the reorganization continued. If it did, the causal argument substantially strengthens.

Post-implementation H-2B trends. We have H-2B data through FY2022. FY2023-24 would show whether the surge sustained or plateaued once firms had built their pipelines.

Cross-state generalizability. We study two counties in one state. The analogous enforcement literature (SB1070, HB87) provides the broader context. Our contribution is rigorous county-level evidence from a specific high-exposure geography using real administrative data.

Honest limits
What we can and cannot claim

Research that overstates its findings loses credibility at the moment it matters most.

We can claim with confidence: The fragmentation pattern is real and present in two independent sources (BDS establishment counts; QWI full-quarter employment share). H-2B positions surged before the law passed and scale with exposure across all 67 Florida counties (p<0.001). The LFP gap exists in ACS data and is directionally consistent with the interior enforcement literature. The community did not disperse. None of these findings is explained by Hurricane Ian or by pre-existing trends (placebo: beta=-0.0007, n.s.).
We cannot claim: That the LFP gap mechanism is definitively enforcement fear. That the establishment surge would not have occurred absent SB1718 (2024 BDS will test). That H-2B growth is exclusively SB1718-driven rather than partly reflecting post-COVID national demand. That findings generalize beyond Sarasota and Manatee to other enforcement contexts or geographies.
The most important next step: Systematic qualitative evidence collection by UnidosNow. The quantitative signal is documented across five independent data sources. The mechanism connecting enforcement salience to worker behavior requires community-level evidence that no state agency, research institution, or academic partner can collect credibly -- because none of them hold the trust relationships that make honest disclosure possible. That is UnidosNow's distinctive capacity and the most valuable investment they can make in this evidentiary base.
